> CHRONO_WIKI // INDEX

Started as a technical archive on how machines keep time, and how that keeping breaks. Still is, mostly - but a salvage archive picks up other things along the way too. Short entries, not exhaustive essays: trivia and dumb conversation-enders, filed here because they didn't fit anywhere else on the ship.

> The Unix Epoch & ISO 8601, a primer

Lives on the Date Stamp Recon page itself rather than duplicated here - the short version: machines count seconds from January 1, 1970, 00:00:00 UTC, and everything since is an incrementing integer. ISO 8601 is the closest thing to a universal human-readable format for the same information.

> The Year 2038 Problem

A lot of systems store that Unix timestamp as a signed 32-bit integer. That format runs out of road on January 19, 2038, at 03:14:07 UTC - one second later, the count wraps back around to a large negative number, which most software reads as December 1901. Same failure mode as the Y2K panic, different root cause: Y2K was a storage-shortcut problem (two-digit years), this is an arithmetic-overflow problem (a fixed-width integer running out of bits). Most modern 64-bit systems already sidestepped it by widening the integer. Plenty of embedded and legacy 32-bit systems have not, and won't notice until they do.

> Timezone Offsets, and Why They Ruin Everything

Timezones aren't clean hour-wide slices - India runs UTC+5:30, Nepal runs UTC+5:45, and some Pacific islands hop entire calendar days depending which side of the International Date Line their government prefers this decade. Add daylight saving time (which not every country observes, and which some countries have started and then abandoned more than once) and "what time is it in the other office" stops being arithmetic and starts being a lookup against a constantly-revised political database. This is also why Date Stamp Recon always shows UTC first and local time second, not the other way around - UTC doesn't change its mind.

> Leap Seconds

Earth's rotation is slowing down, almost imperceptibly, so every so often an official leap second gets inserted to keep clock time lined up with the actual planet. It sounds harmless. In 2012, a leap second exposed a bug in the Linux kernel's futex (fast userspace mutex) implementation that pushed CPU usage on affected servers to 100% - Reddit, Mozilla, and Yelp all had a bad night over one extra second. Several major tech companies now "smear" the leap second across an entire day of very slightly slower ticks instead of inserting it all at once, specifically so this doesn't happen again.

> Why Cron Starts the Week on Sunday

Cron's day-of-week field runs 0-6 with Sunday as 0, not Monday - which trips up anyone used to a Monday-first calendar. It's inherited from POSIX C's struct tm, where tm_wday has always used the same 0-for-Sunday convention, which itself traces back to older US calendar conventions treating Sunday as the start of the week. Cron didn't invent the choice, it just never had a reason to break from the C standard library it was already built on top of.

> Where "Foo" and "Bar" Came From

The most commonly cited origin traces "foo" and "bar" back to military slang from WWII - specifically FUBAR ("[fouled] up beyond all recognition") - which filtered into early hacker culture (MIT's Tech Model Railroad Club and, later, ARPANET documentation) as a placeholder pair for "some arbitrary thing" and "some other arbitrary thing." The etymology is debated in the details, but the FUBAR connection is the explanation the Jargon File itself settles on. Nobody remembers who used it first in code; everybody's used it since.

> The Odds a UUID Ever Repeats

A version-4 UUID has 122 genuinely random bits (the other 6 are fixed, marking it as version 4). Birthday-paradox math says you'd need to generate roughly 2.71 quintillion of them before the odds of any two colliding crossed 50%. Every UUID this site has ever generated, and every one it ever will, is functionally guaranteed unique - not because the math forbids a collision, but because the universe will have other problems long before that number of UUIDs gets generated by anyone.
