> Why There's No Paywall-Bypass Tool Here

A specific category of tool exists purely to get around a publisher's paywall. They work. They also directly contradict the terms the publisher set for their own content, which is a different thing from reading an old cached copy of a page that's since changed. This is a deliberate line, not an oversight - if a category of link consistently shows up in every "secret websites" list and consistently doesn't show up here, this is why.

Not all paywalls are the same wall

A hard paywall blocks the article entirely without a subscription (most of the Wall Street Journal). A metered/soft paywall gives a set number of free articles before cutting you off, usually tracked by a cookie or a JavaScript counter (most general-interest newspapers). A freemium model gives away most content and paywalls only specific premium pieces. The metered kind is the one bypass tools target most, precisely because the mechanism enforcing it often lives entirely in the browser - a cookie, a bit of client-side script - rather than on the server, which is what makes it technically easy to defeat and legally risky anyway, since "easy to defeat" and "permitted to defeat" are unrelated questions.

This isn't a hypothetical legal question

In August 2024, GitHub took down a popular paywall-bypassing browser extension and its thousands of forks after a DMCA complaint from the News Media Alliance, a trade group representing over 2,200 news and media publishers. The complaint's legal basis wasn't ordinary copyright infringement - it specifically invoked Section 1201 of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, the anti-circumvention provision that makes it illegal to bypass a technical measure protecting copyrighted content, separate from and in addition to any claim about the content itself. US courts have separately upheld those anti-circumvention provisions as constitutional. That's the live legal environment a "just add a paywall bypasser" feature would actually be shipping into, not an abstract risk.

The honest context

Industry research consistently finds that only a small minority of readers globally - commonly cited around 15-20% - actually pay for online news, and a large majority of readers report simply avoiding paywalled sites rather than paying or bypassing them. Paywalls are, for most publishers, one of the few subscription-revenue mechanisms left standing as ad revenue keeps shrinking industry-wide - which doesn't make every paywall well-designed or every publisher's terms fair, but it does mean "just make the content free again" isn't a neutral position, it's taking a side in a funding fight that's already squeezing the newsroom producing the reporting in the first place. Reading an already-archived, already-public snapshot of a page (see link rot & digital preservation) is a different act entirely from a live tool built to defeat a paywall the moment it's put up - the former deals with what's already public, the latter exists to make something non-public anyway.
